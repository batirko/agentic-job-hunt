# PART A: HOW SENIORITY IS ACTUALLY DETECTED

## 2) What interviewers are measuring (and the probe mechanic)

An interviewer cannot observe nine years of judgment in 45 minutes. So they sample it. They pick one of your decisions and drill down until they hit either bedrock (real reasoning, real tradeoffs, real consequences you carried) or air (rehearsed surface, framework recitation, borrowed credit). The level they assign you is the depth at which your answers stay substantive.

**The three depths of a probe:**

| Depth | Question shape | What survives it |
|-------|---------------|------------------|
| 1 | "How do you prioritize?" "Tell me about a conflict." | Anyone with a prepared answer |
| 2 | "Why that order and not the reverse?" "Who disagreed, and what was their actual argument?" | People who genuinely made the decision |
| 3 | "What did that choice cost you?" "What would make you reverse it today?" "What changed in how you work because of it?" | People who owned the consequences |

Junior candidates are indistinguishable from senior candidates at depth 1. The separation happens at depths 2 and 3. This has one huge practical implication: **prepare depth, not breadth.** Five stories you can defend three levels down beat fifteen stories you can only open. Your story bank already has the raw material; what it needs is the depth tree behind each story (section 16).

**The four currencies interviewers convert into a level:**

1. **Ownership language.** Decisions have an owner, and in your stories it was you. "I decided, against X's preference, and here is why" reads senior. "It was decided" and "we felt" read junior even when the underlying work was excellent. The senior pattern: "I" for decisions and mistakes, "we" for execution and credit.
2. **Tradeoff visibility.** Real decisions cost something. Seniors name the cost unprompted: what got slower, who was unhappy, what risk was accepted. A decision presented as a free win signals that you either didn't see the cost or didn't carry it. Both read junior.
3. **Calibration.** Knowing the confidence level of your own claims. "High confidence, we had production data" and "honestly, that was a judgment call on thin evidence, here is why I still made it" both read senior. Uniform confidence across everything reads junior. "I don't know, here is how I would find out" is a senior answer, and refusing to say it is a junior tell.
4. **Altitude control.** Deliberately moving between strategy and detail, and signaling the moves: "at the strategy level the question was X; in execution that meant Y; let me stay at the strategy level unless you want the detail." Juniors get stuck at one altitude, usually the detail. Seniors choose.

**The level ladder, in answer terms:**

- **Junior:** narrates process. "We ran discovery, wrote a PRD, groomed the backlog."
- **Mid:** narrates execution. "I shipped X, it did Y."
- **Senior:** narrates decisions. "The real question was A versus B. I chose A, it cost us C, here is the result and what I'd do differently."
- **Staff:** narrates systems. "After solving it once, I changed the mechanism so it never needed me again."

Here is the part that should change how you close every story: **your story bank is probably full of staff-level endings you are not using.** The grading scheme that became someone else's owned metric. The rules another team now publishes by. The contract other teams build on. The review you installed after it was too late. Every one of those is "I changed the system, not just the instance." End stories there, not at the metric.

## 3) Signals and anti-signals

What leaks seniority upward:

- Opening with the decision principle, then the story ("My rule for X is Y. Here's a case where it got tested.")
- Naming the counterparty's argument better than they made it, then explaining why you still decided differently
- Numbers attached to outcomes, with honest caveats about attribution
- Unprompted "what I'd do differently," specific and structural, not cosmetic
- Explicit escalation logic: what you decide alone, what you decide with input, what you hand up
- Asking the interviewer a sharp question back when the case is underspecified ("was this pre or post PMF? That changes my answer")

What leaks juniority, even from strong candidates:

- Framework name-dropping as the answer ("I use RICE") instead of as vocabulary
- Passive voice around decisions ("it was deprioritized")
- Zero-cost decisions: every story ends in a win with no bill attached
- Blaming stakeholders, sales, "politics," or the org in conflict stories
- Uniform confidence; never saying "I don't know"
- Detail flooding: 4 minutes of context before any decision appears (**your confirmed failure mode, flagged by name by two companies**)
- Opening an example with a system, a product state, or an org size instead of a person in a concrete bad moment. The interviewer cannot tell why anything mattered, so nothing that follows can land
- Burying the differentiated insight in minute three. It scores as absent, because the interviewer grades what they could follow
- Asking-permission energy: "I would check with leadership" as the reflexive answer to every hard call
- Treating alignment as the goal rather than the means ("I got everyone aligned" with no mention of what the decision was)

# PART B: THE FOUR PROBE DOMAINS

## 4) Prioritization

**What the question is actually measuring.** Never the framework. It measures whether you have a decision system you can articulate, whether you can say no to powerful people, whether you understand opportunity cost, and whether your roadmap survives contact with reality without thrashing.

**The senior mental model of prioritization, in six load-bearing ideas:**

1. **Strategy filters, evidence sizes, frameworks tie-break.** Strategy decides which problems are allowed into the conversation at all. Evidence (usage data, research, deal signal) sizes the ones that pass. Scoring frameworks exist to make the ranking arguable in public and to break ties. A PM whose prioritization begins at the scoring spreadsheet has outsourced the actual decision.
2. **Every yes is a no somewhere else.** Opportunity cost is the real unit of prioritization. Senior answers name what was displaced: "we did the Bitbucket integration, which meant the IDE roadmap sat for a quarter."
3. **Cost of delay orders the queue.** Two items of equal value are not equal if one decays. Deal-gating work, compliance deadlines, and trust-erosion problems (bad data in a catalog) all carry decay. Sequencing by decay, not just by size, is a senior tell.
4. **Reversibility sets decision speed.** Cheap reversible bets should be made fast and sloppy; expensive irreversible ones slowly and with evidence. Naming which type you were facing, and matching your process to it, reads senior. Agonizing over a reversible call reads as junior as rushing an irreversible one.
5. **Capacity is a portfolio.** Run the platform, grow adoption, place bets. A roadmap that is 100% feature bets has no infrastructure tax budgeted, and you have lived the consequence: even mature, "stable" surfaces need budget or they rot. Saying that sentence is a senior tell in any tech-debt question.
6. **Re-prioritization has triggers, not moods.** The expensive failure mode is thrash. Seniors can state what would change their ranking mid-quarter (a deal signal above a threshold, a trust-breaking quality issue, a dependency slip) and what would not (louder repetition of an old request).

**How the probes are phrased:**

- "How do you prioritize?" / "Walk me through your roadmap process."
- "Two stakeholders both call their item P0. What do you do?"
- "Your biggest customer demands something not on the roadmap."
- "How do you balance tech debt against features?"
- "Tell me about a time you said no. To whom, and what happened?"
- "What did you deliberately not build?" (the strongest version of the question; have a real answer)
- "When did you change a priority mid-cycle, and how did you communicate it?"

**Worked contrast.** Question: "How do you prioritize?"

*Junior version:* "I use RICE. I score reach, impact, confidence and effort, rank the backlog, and review it with stakeholders every sprint." (Describes administration. No strategy, no conflict, no case where the system broke.)

*Senior version:* **write your own here.** It has to do four things the junior version does not: state the principle before the example, attach a specific person or team, make the decision yours with the rejected option in the same sentence, and name what the choice cost. If your draft is missing any of the four, it will be scored as competent and unremarkable.

**Traps:**

- The framework trap: being fluent in RICE/WSJF vocabulary and mistaking that for an answer. Use framework names the way you'd cite a tool, never as the verdict.
- The no-conflict trap: describing prioritization as if inputs arrive, get scored, and everyone nods. Interviewers know it never works that way; a frictionless story reads fictional.
- The fake-certainty trap: claiming the data made the decision. Data informs; you decided. Own the judgment gap between the two.
- The infinite-diplomacy trap: answering every conflict question with "I'd facilitate a workshop to align." Sometimes the senior answer is "I decided, informed everyone, and took the heat."

> **FILL IN: your lead stories for this domain.** Two or three, each one you can defend three questions deep. Name the story, and in brackets the beat that makes it senior rather than competent.

## 5) Customers and problems

**What the question is actually measuring.** Whether you can find the real problem underneath what customers say, whether you weight evidence by strength rather than volume, whether you can say no to a customer and keep the relationship, and whether your discovery is a habit or a ceremony.

**The senior mental model, in six ideas:**

1. **The evidence hierarchy.** What people do beats what they pay for, which beats what they say, which beats what they say they would do. Senior answers always name the evidence type behind a claim and its weakness: "interviews suggested X, but interviews overweight articulate users, so we confirmed against usage data."
2. **Customers bring solutions; the job is decoding them to problems.** Users asked you for better filters. Research showed the navigation model itself contradicted their mental model, and the fix was intent-based discovery, not filter improvements. That story is the cleanest possible proof you understand problem space versus solution space. Lead with it.
3. **Discovery finds problems; validation tests solutions. Different activities, different rigor.** Conflating them ("we validated with five interviews") is a junior tell. Seniors also know when to skip: a cheap reversible bet sometimes ships without a study, and saying so shows judgment rather than dogma.
4. **Feedback is weighted by segment strategy, not by volume or volume of voice.** The loudest-customer trap and the biggest-customer trap are the same trap at different price points. The question underneath: does this request generalize to the segment we are betting on, or is it one account's workflow wearing a feature request as a costume?
5. **Saying no to a customer has a shape:** acknowledge the underlying problem as real, explain the frame you prioritize within, give a path (a smaller version, a timeline condition, a workaround, or an honest "not us, and here's who"). The no lands if the customer feels decoded rather than dismissed.
6. **The buyer and the user are different customers with different jobs.** In most B2B and platform contexts this split is acute: one party signs, another lives with the result and can quietly refuse to adopt it. Senior PMs in B2B name this split without being asked and can describe how they serve both without confusing them.

**Your structural advantage, stated plainly (use this):** your internal customers were volunteers. No contract, no sales team, and no lock-in stood between your product and churn; engineers who found the portal wasteful would go back to grep, spreadsheets, and tribal knowledge, and the platform would die quietly while dashboards stayed green. Adoption earned per user, per week, is the purest customer game a PM can play. Never let "internal" be heard as "captive."

**How the probes are phrased:**

- "How do you know what customers actually need?"
- "Tell me about a time customer feedback changed your roadmap." (and the mirror: "a time you ignored customer feedback and were right, or wrong")
- "Your top account threatens to churn over a missing feature."
- "How do you do discovery when you can't talk to users directly?"
- "Tell me about a product decision where the data and your intuition disagreed."
- "How do you decide which feedback to act on?"

**Worked contrast.** Question: "A big customer demands a feature that doesn't fit the roadmap."

*Junior version:* "I'd evaluate the request against our prioritization framework and communicate the decision transparently." (No decoding, no dollar awareness, no relationship management, no path.)

*Senior version:* **write your own here.** It has to do four things the junior version does not: state the principle before the example, attach a specific person or team, make the decision yours with the rejected option in the same sentence, and name what the choice cost. If your draft is missing any of the four, it will be scored as competent and unremarkable.

**Traps:**

- Quoting "we talked to customers" without being able to say who, how many, how selected, and what would have falsified the conclusion. Depth-2 kills this instantly.
- Treating NPS or a feature-vote board as discovery.
- Hero-listening: stories where a single customer conversation revealed everything. Reads lucky, not senior.
- Forgetting the economics: in enterprise contexts, customer questions are partly revenue questions, and answering them with pure UX-research vocabulary misses half the game.

> **FILL IN: your lead stories for this domain.** Two or three, each one you can defend three questions deep. Name the story, and in brackets the beat that makes it senior rather than competent.

## 6) Stakeholders and influence

**What the question is actually measuring.** Whether you design decisions or merely attend them. Whether you can move people who don't report to you. Whether your manager could delegate ambiguity to you and sleep. And in conflict stories, one thing above all: whether the other party comes out of your telling as a reasonable person with a real constraint, or as the villain. Blame is the fastest seniority-killer in this domain.

**The senior mental model, in seven ideas:**

1. **Stakeholder management is decision design, not meeting survival.** Before content, settle mechanics: who decides, who must be consulted, who is merely informed, and by when. DACI is fine vocabulary, but the senior version is the habit, not the acronym: "the first thing I do in a contested decision is get agreement on who owns it."
2. **Pre-wiring.** Big meetings ratify decisions; they don't make them. Walk the decision through each key player one-on-one first, absorb objections while they're cheap, adjust, and arrive at the meeting with the coalition already built. If a decision is getting made in the room, in front of everyone, you've already lost control of it.
3. **Managing up means selling decisions, not problems, and never surprises.** The format that works: here's the situation, here are the options with their costs, here's my recommendation, I need your call only on X by date D or the default is Y. Escalation done this way is a tool, not a failure; escalation done as "help, they won't align" is a demotion request.
4. **Most disagreements are about weights, not facts.** Separate them: usually both sides accept the same data and weight it differently because their incentives differ. Naming that ("we agree on the numbers; you're weighting deal risk, I'm weighting platform repeatability") de-personalizes the conflict and often dissolves it. When it doesn't, timebox, decide or escalate, and commit.
5. **Disagree and commit runs in both directions.** You commit genuinely to decisions that went against you (no relitigating, no slow-rolling, no told-you-so on failure), and you spend the credibility that earns you on the decisions where you overrule others. Have one story of each direction ready.
6. **The exec pet feature (the HiPPO) gets converted, not blocked.** Turn the opinion into a testable claim, find the cheapest experiment that could kill it, and give the exec a graceful exit. Your search-widget-before-chatbot sequencing is literally this: the pressure was to ship the flashy chatbot; you gave stakeholders an iterative story where AI value shipped early, instead of a refusal.
7. **Engineering is a stakeholder you win by respecting constraints, not negotiating estimates.** Bring problems and context, not specs. Cut scope yourself before asking for speed. Budget the infrastructure tax without being asked. You have an unusual advantage here: your users were engineers, your product was for engineers, and catalog quality became a team-owned metric because you designed accountability instead of mandates. Teams respond to accountability, not rankings: that sentence is yours, use it.

**How the probes are phrased:**

- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with engineering / design / sales / your manager."
- "How do you influence without authority?"
- "Tell me about a stakeholder who was blocking you."
- "A decision went against you. What did you do?"
- "How do you handle an executive who wants their idea shipped?"
- "Tell me about a time you had to deliver an unpopular decision."
- "How do you keep leadership informed?" (this is a cadence-and-trust question, not a reporting question)

**Worked contrast.** Question: "Tell me about a time a stakeholder pushed back hard."

*Junior version:* "Sales kept escalating a customer request. I explained our prioritization framework and eventually leadership backed the roadmap." (Villain identified, framework as shield, authority as resolution. Three junior tells in two sentences.)

*Senior version:* **write your own here.** It has to do four things the junior version does not: state the principle before the example, attach a specific person or team, make the decision yours with the rejected option in the same sentence, and name what the choice cost. If your draft is missing any of the four, it will be scored as competent and unremarkable.

**Traps:**

- Any story where the counterpart is irrational, political, or stupid. Interviewers side with the absent party by default.
- "I aligned everyone" with no decision visible. Alignment is a means; name what was decided and who decided.
- Escalating as the first move, or never escalating: both read junior. The senior answer names the escalation threshold.
- Claiming influence through relationships alone ("I'd grab a coffee with them"). Coffee is a channel, not a strategy.

> **FILL IN: your lead stories for this domain.** Two or three, each one you can defend three questions deep. Name the story, and in brackets the beat that makes it senior rather than competent.

## 7) Problems, crises, and failure

**What the question is actually measuring.** Calibration and the learning loop. Whether your failures had real stakes (a failure that cost nothing teaches nothing and signals padded ownership). Whether you deliver bad news early or manage it cosmetically. Whether the lesson changed your operating system or just your slideware.

**The senior mental model, in four ideas:**

1. **Bad news travels first class.** Early, with an owner, an impact estimate, and options. The story to have ready is one where you surfaced a problem before you were forced to, it was uncomfortable, and it went fine because early bad news is cheap and late bad news is fatal. Never tell a story where the problem "emerged."
2. **A real failure story has three parts:** the decision that was actually wrong (yours, not the org's), the cost in concrete terms, and the structural change. The test is whether the failure has a real bill attached, whether you own it rather than the org, and whether the fix is structural rather than "I learned to communicate better".
3. **Kill criteria beat sunk-cost debates.** The senior move is deciding in advance what evidence would kill the project, so killing it later is execution rather than heartbreak. If you've never killed anything, prioritization questions will eventually expose it.
4. **In a crisis, cadence is the product.** Overcommunicate on a fixed rhythm, separate what's known from what's suspected, and repair trust explicitly afterward. For a platform PM the crisis version is usually trust erosion (bad catalog data, a broken golden path) rather than an outage, and the senior insight is that trust degrades silently: dashboards stay green while users route around you.

**How the probes are phrased:**

- "Tell me about your biggest failure." / "A product decision you got wrong."
- "Tell me about a launch that went badly."
- "When did you kill a project, and how did you decide?"
- "Tell me about receiving hard feedback."
- "What's the hardest message you've had to deliver?"

**Traps:**

- The humble-brag failure ("I worked too hard, we shipped too much"). Instantly disqualifying at senior level.
- The consequence-free failure. If nothing was lost, it wasn't a failure, it was a Tuesday.
- Lessons that are adjectives ("I learned to be more proactive"). Senior lessons are mechanisms ("I now install a standing commercial-signal review in week one").
- Blame leaking in through the back door ("my failure was trusting the estimates"). That's an accusation in a trench coat.

> **FILL IN: your lead stories for this domain.** Two or three, each one you can defend three questions deep. Name the story, and in brackets the beat that makes it senior rather than competent.

# PART C: WHO IS PROBING, AND WHAT THEY'RE AFRAID OF

## 8) The interviewer role map

Every interviewer converts your answers into an answer to one private question of their own. Aim the same stories at different private questions.

| Interviewer | Their private question | Currency that convinces | Classic probe |
|-------------|------------------------|-------------------------|---------------|
| Recruiter | "Is this person the level on the req, and will they survive the panel?" | Crisp scope claims, clean narrative, no red flags, comp alignment | "Walk me through your background" (they're listening for level markers, not content) |
| Hiring manager | "Can I delegate ambiguity to this person and sleep?" | A visible operating system: cadence, escalation thresholds, no-surprises instinct | "How would you spend your first 90 days?" and any disagree-with-manager story |
| Peer / principal PM | "Is the depth real, and will they respect scope boundaries?" | Depth-3 survival, intellectual honesty, opinions held loosely, zero bluffing | "Walk me through one decision in painful detail" |
| Engineering leader | "Will this PM respect constraints, or negotiate my estimates and blame us later?" | Tradeoff literacy, self-initiated scope cuts, budgeted tech debt, technical fluency without cosplay | "How do you handle a slipping deadline?" "Tech debt vs features?" |
| Design / research | "Will evidence actually change this person's mind?" | Evidence-hierarchy fluency, a story where research overturned your own plan | "Tell me about research changing a decision" |
| Sales / CS leader | "Can they say no to my deal without burning it, and will the roadmap ever help me?" | Deal-language fluency, a no-with-a-path story, standing commercial intake | "Big account demands X. Go." |
| Founder / exec | "Do they have taste, speed, and conviction, or do they need an org around them?" | Opinions about THEIR product with reasoning, business-model fluency, fast updates when challenged | "What would you change about our product?" "What would you push back on?" |
| Bar raiser / values | "Are the stories consistent, and is the reflection real?" | Same story details under re-asking, failures with real stakes | Re-asks of earlier questions from new angles |

**Role-specific notes for your live processes:**

- **Recruiter screens.** Level is decided by scope statements, so deliver them pre-compressed: what you own, who builds on it, what you decide alone, one number. Twenty seconds, four level markers, done. Do not narrate projects at a recruiter; narrate scope.
- **Peer / principal technical interviews.** The bluff detector is strongest here; principals interview the way they review specs. Pick your single deepest story and volunteer the ugly parts (retrofit work, the churned accounts, the research you ran too late). Peers upgrade you for scars. When asked about their product, give an opinion with a reasoning chain and an explicit uncertainty: "from outside, I'd guess the false-positive budget matters more than detection breadth, but you have the data and I don't." That posture (opinionated, calibrated, non-territorial) is exactly what a principal wants in a peer.
- **Founder interviews.** Founders probe conviction and speed, and they fear hiring a process-shaped person who needs a big company to function. Lead with your most greenfield story, framed as founder-mode-inside-constraints. Have two or three product opinions about their product, stated plainly, updated fast under pushback. A founder would rather hire someone wrong and fast than right and slow, because they can argue with wrong.

# PART D: HOW COMPANY TYPE AND STAGE CHANGE THE GAME

## 9) The context lens

"Senior" is not one thing. Each company context pays in a different currency, fears a different failure mode in candidates, and asks the same three probe domains from a different angle. Read your target before choosing which face of your experience leads.

### Early startup (pre-PMF, seed to A)

- **Prioritization means:** deciding what NOT to build, killing fast, betting under near-zero data. Frameworks read as overhead.
- **Customers means:** the PM talks to users personally, weekly. Delegated discovery is a red flag.
- **Stakeholders means:** the founder, period. Managing a founder's conviction is the whole game.
- **They fear:** the big-company person who needs process, headcount, and a quarter of alignment before shipping.

# PART E: THE VOCABULARY LAYER

## 10) Frameworks and mental models (and the meta-rule)

**The meta-rule first: frameworks are communication devices, not decision engines.** A senior PM uses them to make reasoning arguable in public, compress a discussion, or tie-break. The moment a framework is the answer to "why," you've been caught outsourcing judgment. The senior sentence shape is: "the decision came from X; I used framework Y to socialize it."

You don't need depth in all of these. You need to recognize them, place them, and know the trap of each, so nothing an interviewer says lands as a surprise and nothing you say sounds like a course certificate.

### Strategy

| Model | One line | Reach for it when | Trap |
|-------|----------|-------------------|------|
| Strategy kernel (Rumelt) | Diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action; strategy is a filter, not a goal list | "What's your product strategy?" or critiquing a vague one | Reciting it instead of having one |
| Playing to Win | Where to play, how to win, as linked choices | Positioning and segment questions | Treating it as a form to fill |
| Positioning (April Dunford flavor) | What you're best at, for whom, against what alternative | PLG and dev-tools interviews | Confusing positioning with messaging |
| Moats / compounding advantage | Network effects, switching costs, ecosystem lock-in | Founder interviews, "why will this win" | Claiming moats a product doesn't have |

### Prioritization

## 13) Answer architecture

**The six-beat senior answer (60 to 90 seconds, then offer depth):**

1. **Principle** (~10s, one sentence): "My rule for X is Y." This beat is what makes an answer non-obvious inside the first ten seconds. Skipping it is how a strong answer gets scored as a competent-but-unremarkable one.
2. **Customer** (~20s): a specific person or team, the concrete friction they lived with, and how you knew. Not the system, not the org chart, not the product state. "A team onboarding a new service filed tickets across three teams and waited two weeks before they could deploy." Name the evidence type in passing ("we watched it in the research sessions").
3. **Decision** (~30s): the options that existed, the call you made, "I" attached to it. Not a summary of activity ("ran discovery, led execution"), which is the compression that hides the decision entirely.
4. **Cost** (~10s): what the choice displaced or risked. This beat is the one juniors skip; never skip it.
5. **Result** (~10s): the number, with an honest attribution caveat if needed.
6. **System change** (~10s): what's now permanent in how you operate.

Then: "Happy to go deeper on the discovery, the politics, or the numbers." Offering the depth menu signals you have all three floors and lets the interviewer drill where they care. Seniors control the clock; a five-minute answer to a depth-1 question reads junior regardless of content.

**Why beat 2 exists.** The original architecture went principle, case, cost, result, system change, and the customer never appeared as its own beat. One rejection landed on exactly that: the customer context was not consistently clear. For internal platform work the trap is sharper, because the honest, precise framing is "internal engineering teams, not customers," and precision quietly becomes permission to open with an architecture instead of a person. Be precise about what they are called. Still open with one of them in a bad moment.

**Beats 1 and 2 are the ones that fail under pressure**, because both feel like preamble when you are nervous and want to get to the substance. They are not preamble. Beat 1 is the differentiation and beat 2 is the thread. Lose them and the interviewer hears a competent, hard-to-follow recital of things that happened, which is precisely what four companies have now reported back.

**Calibration phrases (use several per interview, deliberately):**

- "High confidence on this one; we had production data."
- "That part was a judgment call on thin evidence. Here's why I still made it."
- "I don't know. Here's how I'd find out, and what I'd expect."
- "From outside your product I'd guess X, but you have the data and I don't."

**Handling live pushback (this IS the test, especially from principals and founders):**
Steel-man first ("so your concern is that X, and honestly the strongest version of that argument is Y"). Then either update visibly ("that changes my answer, because I was assuming Z") or hold with reasons ("I still land on A, and here's the evidence that would move me"). The two failure modes are folding instantly (no conviction) and digging in (no coachability). What's being graded is the update mechanism, not the position.
